# Visual arts

Use this shared domain reference when the learner wants to develop practical capability in making visual art, including critique or revision in service of making. It may support drawing, painting, illustration, calligraphy, sculpture, ceramics, textiles, and related visual or material practices. Do not use it for art history, aesthetics, museum study, or art criticism without a making-skill goal, or for image generation, image editing, or finished-art production without a learning goal.

## Identify the capability

Identify the main capability before choosing exercises, critique, or aids. Use relevant categories such as perceptual discrimination; representation and simplification; spatial or form reasoning; mark or material control; value and light; color; composition and hierarchy; reference or model use; generation, transformation, or stylization; process planning; critique and revision; convention or tradition; and adaptation or transfer.

Do not treat these categories as a mandatory curriculum sequence. If several matter, name the principal current target so assistance does not replace the operation being practiced.

## Separate kinds of judgment

Distinguish:

- **technical or relational:** observable relationships or task constraints;
- **goal-dependent:** whether a choice supports the learner's stated intention; and
- **aesthetic or interpretive:** preference, expression, or competing artistic possibilities.

Tie technical claims to observable evidence and goal-dependent claims to the stated goal. Present aesthetic judgments as interpretations or options, not objective rules. When a convention belongs to a named tradition, do not generalize it into a universal law of good art.

## Select practice by capability

Choose practice that exercises the target operation. Useful forms include focused observation, construction, constrained studies, example or master studies, reconstruction, variation, original application, critique and revision, and delayed or changed-context application.

Treat these as domain options, not a sequence. Use the adaptive teaching workflow to select the acquisition strategy, scaffolding, fading, and retrieval pattern. Prefer exercises whose relationship to the target visual capability can be explained.

## Use references deliberately

Treat reference use as normal. Before assigning copying or a master study, identify what the learner should notice or practice, such as composition, simplification, line, value, color, form, mark language, or convention.

When transfer matters, follow direct study with reconstruction, comparison, changed application, or original use of the extracted principle. Do not assume that copying automatically teaches technique or that avoiding references produces originality.

## Protect the independence boundary

Judge tools and aids by the capability being learned, not by a global label such as legitimate, cheating, educational, or harmful. An aid is compatible when it supports surrounding work without replacing the target operation; reduce or remove it when independent performance of that delegated operation is itself the target.

Examples:

- perspective grids may support composition or rendering but substitute for independent perspective construction;
- tracing may support stroke study or analysis but substitute for observational proportion;
- stabilizers may support shape design but substitute for freehand mark control;
- color sampling may support composition but substitute for visual color matching;
- 3D blockouts may support lighting or rendering but substitute for independent spatial construction;
- generated alternatives may support evaluation or transformation but substitute for independent ideation; and
- external critique may support revision but substitute for independent error detection.

Do not impose tool abstinence when tool-augmented performance is the practical capability the learner wants.

## Make critique diagnostic

Establish or infer the intended outcome and current capability target. Prioritize a small number of consequential issues rather than producing an exhaustive defect list.

For each important issue, connect **observation -> why it matters -> plausible correction or experiment**.

Do not imply that AI critique is exhaustive. When critique judgment is itself being learned, give the learner opportunities to find missed problems, reject poor advice, or defend a legitimate alternative.

## Build curricula backward from capability

For an art-learning plan, translate what the learner wants to make or do into relevant visual capabilities, prerequisites, and bottlenecks. Combine focused studies with meaningful artistic applications. Leave general sequencing, scaffolding, revisiting, and assessment to the adaptive teaching workflow.

Do not substitute a conventional academic sequence merely because it is common. Do not require portraiture, photorealism, figure drawing, or another traditional subject unless it serves the learner's goal or an identified prerequisite.

## Evaluate progress beyond artifact quality

For visual-art transfer, use probes such as a changed subject, unfamiliar reference, delayed redraw, altered constraint, self-diagnosis, or correction of a recurring visual problem. Treat a better assisted artifact as evidence about the artifact, not by itself about retained capability; apply the core workflow's mastery standard.

Do not assume cross-medium transfer. Test it when it matters.

## Handle unsupported media carefully

When no narrower family reference exists, apply only the shared rules that remain relevant. For medium-specific execution, technique, safety, historical convention, or material behavior, use verified subject-matter information under `teach-me`'s normal research rules.

Do not import drawing or painting procedures into ceramics, sculpture, weaving, or another practice merely because both are visual arts.

## Preserve cultural and stylistic specificity

When the learner names a tradition, school, script, genre, or culturally specific practice, preserve meaningful distinctions. Research factual claims about unfamiliar historical practice, terminology, technique, or convention when verification could materially affect the lesson.

Do not imply that one tradition's conventions are universal aesthetic laws.

## Avoid unsupported doctrine

Do not assert that there is one optimal sequence of art fundamentals; artists learn by seeing a purely retinal image; motor control primarily explains drawing skill; copying automatically develops technique; references undermine originality; critique has one universally optimal format; feedback should follow a fixed fading schedule; traditional or digital media are inherently superior for fundamentals; learning transfers automatically between media; tracing, grids, 3D tools, stabilizers, or AI are inherently good or bad for learning; realism must precede stylization; or polished assisted work demonstrates mastery.
