# Drawing, painting, and calligraphy

Read this after `visual-arts.md` when the learner is developing capability in 2D mark- or image-making, including drawing, sketching, painting, digital painting, illustration, ink work, watercolor, concept art, visual development, comics-related image making, lettering where drawing principles dominate, or calligraphy. Apply it from the actual learning task rather than the software or physical medium.

Do not load it for sculpture, ceramics, weaving, or another materially different practice unless the request also contains a genuine drawing or painting learning component.

## Map fundamentals to the goal

When the learner asks for fundamentals, select among observation and proportion; shape and simplification; form and perspective; mark and edge control; value and light; color; composition; reference use; generation and stylization; critique and revision. Weight them according to what the learner wants to make instead of returning a universal checklist.

## Teach observation as active comparison

Direct attention toward useful relationships such as relative proportion, angles and alignment, positive and negative shapes, overlap, contour, value, edges, and spatial cues. Use measuring or comparison aids as scaffolds when useful; when independent observation is the target, later test whether the learner detects consequential relationships without automatic comparison.

Do not describe expert observation as literal access to an unbiased retinal image.

## Build spatial construction when required

For perspective, environments, objects, creatures, or imagined forms, develop reasoning about volume, orientation, overlap, scale, planes, axes, perspective, and changed viewpoints rather than memorized outlines. Use simple forms, reference rotation, perspective tools, or 3D aids according to the independence boundary.

A learner may competently use a perspective grid professionally while separately training independent perspective reasoning.

## Teach value and light as organization

When value matters, develop judgment about light and dark grouping, readability, illumination, form, local value, contrast hierarchy, and rendering. Use constrained-value or grayscale studies when isolating value reduces irrelevant complexity, but do not require grayscale before every color task.

## Teach color when color is a target

Develop hue, value, saturation, temperature, contextual relationships, palette organization, and expressive use as relevant. Do not treat color as a reward that must wait for monochrome mastery. If a color problem is confounded with value, isolate the relevant variable temporarily and then restore color.

## Teach composition early when useful

Use thumbnails, cropping, variants, value masses, focal hierarchy, spacing, scale, rhythm, contrast, or other simplified representations to make compositional decisions cheaply. When composition judgment is the target, prefer generating or comparing multiple plausible alternatives over polishing the first solution.

## Train mark and edge control purposefully

Use line, stroke, pressure, edge, texture, or brush-control exercises when execution limits the intended result. Do not prescribe high-volume motor drills by default. When practical, connect execution practice to meaningful forms or images so control and visual judgment develop together.

Distinguish an execution problem from a perception or construction problem before prescribing more hand-control practice.

## Use master and reference studies analytically

Apply the shared visual-arts reference-use procedure. For drawing or painting, useful study targets include composition, shape abstraction, value grouping, color organization, edge treatment, brush or line language, perspective, visual rhythm, or stylistic transformation. Do not require a full-artwork reproduction when a smaller study exercises the target more efficiently.

## Support imaginative and stylized work directly

Do not require realism as a universal gateway to stylization or imaginative art. Combine relevant observation and construction with transformation when the learner wants to invent landscapes, environments, creatures, objects, characters, or visual languages.

A useful option is **reference -> structural analysis -> simplification or exaggeration -> combination or variation -> novel application**. Use it only when it fits the target capability.

Treat style as deliberate transformation of shape, proportion, line, value, color, edge, texture, spacing, or convention rather than surface imitation alone.

## Critique at the right level

Specialize the shared technical, goal-dependent, and aesthetic judgment types for 2D work by separating:

- **structure:** proportion, spatial relationships, value organization, unintended tangencies, and similar relationships;
- **goal-relative design:** composition, hierarchy, readability, mood, or shape language;
- **execution:** line, edge, brushwork, transitions, or material handling; and
- **aesthetic interpretation:** competing stylistic or expressive possibilities.

Prioritize the issue most likely to improve the current learning target rather than correcting everything visible. If the artwork itself is unavailable, do not pretend to inspect it.

## Keep tool learning proportionate

When tool fluency is the target, teach the operation directly. When the target is artistic capability, introduce only enough software or material technique for productive practice unless additional tool mastery is requested.

When a requested effect could refer to digital or physical media and that choice materially changes execution, identify the intended medium or state a limited assumption before prescribing technique.

Prefer transferable operational concepts such as layers, masks, selections, transforms, blending, brush behavior, canvas navigation, guides, and non-destructive correction where relevant. Give software-specific instructions when needed; tool-agnostic design does not require vague instructions.

## Apply calligraphy guidance conditionally

Use this section only when calligraphy is part of the learning goal. Identify the relevant script, tradition, tool or material context, and desired capability when those distinctions affect instruction.

Consider separately:

- **form and structure:** proportion, spacing, and character or letter construction;
- **stroke procedure:** order and directional conventions where relevant;
- **execution:** pressure, angle, velocity, continuity, rhythm, loading, and release;
- **composition:** spacing, balance, grouping, and page organization;
- **convention:** script- or tradition-specific requirements; and
- **expression:** deliberate variation within or against those conventions.

Do not generalize one calligraphic tradition to another or infer correct stroke procedure from visual similarity alone. Research unfamiliar or consequential claims about script conventions, historical practice, or traditional technique rather than improvising.

## Test rather than assume cross-medium transfer

When the learner switches among pencil, ink, charcoal, watercolor, opaque paint, digital painting, or another 2D medium, identify which capability is intended to carry over and which execution demands changed.

Use the new medium as a transfer probe when useful. If performance drops, distinguish unfamiliar execution from loss of the underlying concept before changing the learning plan.
